    3-D Data Visualisation DP4

    Learning Objectives

    1 Construct interactive 3-D scatter plots using Plotly Express
    2 Map three numerical variables to x-, y- and z-axes
    3 Navigate a 3-D Plotly figure
    4 Customise the axes, labels and appearance of a 3-D Plotly figure
    5 Use 3-D visualisation to explore patterns and subpopulations within multi-marker flow cytometry data
    6 Recognise the limitations of 3-D plots, including occlusion

    Introduction

    Real-world datasets often contain many measurements; a measurement per sample, per cell, per patient or per run of a given experiment. While two-dimensional plots allow us to compare two numerical variables at a time, they can sometimes hide structure that only becomes apparent when a third variable is considered simultaneously. The next step from interactive plots as a way to customise the display of additional data on your plots, is naturally displaying and plotting your data in three dimensions.

    A 3-D plot builds the axes system by adding a third axis referred to as the z-axis, allowing individual points on a plot to be positioned according to three numerical measurements, simultaneously. This can be useful when exploring genuinely multidimensional datasets.

    In this section of Data Processing 4, we will demonstrate 3-D plotting using synthetic flow cytometry data containing measurements for three cell-surface markers: CD3, CD19 and CD56. These markers help distinguish between each of these immune cell populations, and by plotting all three simultaneously, we can explore whether simulated T cells, B cells and natural killer (NK) cells occupy distinct regions of three-dimensional marker-expression space.

    Continuing on from the previous section, we will be constructing our 3-D plots using Plotly, pulling on a familiar syntactical thread, throughout. Three-dimensional visualisations provide the viewer with a multi-perspective experience, as a function of the ability to rotate, zoom and navigate your data, changing the perception of how these points are projected; as opposed to the rather limited perspective that you might get from viewing your data from only a single fixed vantage point.

    Generating synthetic flow cytometry data

    Let us first get acquainted with the dataset we will use in this section of the lesson. For demonstrative purposes, we have decided to use a synthetic flow cytometry dataset that has been generated for you. Synthetic data allow us to reproduce the kinds of patterns found in real-world biological datasets while keeping the underlying cell populations deliberately clear for pedagogical purposes.

    Flow cytometry measures characteristics of individual cells, commonly including the fluorescence associated with labelled proteins on the cell surface. Each row in our dataset therefore represents a single simulated cell, whilst numerical columns describe the expression of three cell-surface markers:

    • CD3 - associated strongly with T cells

    • CD19 - associated strongly with B cells

    • CD56 - commonly associated with natural killer (NK) cells
    The Cell_Type column records the simulated immune cell population to which each observation belongs.

    Our dataset contains 3,000 simulated cells: 1,500 T cells, 1,000 B cells and 500 NK cells. The marker values have been generated so that these populations occupy different, but partially overlapping, regions of marker-expression space.

    The dataset we will make use of here is modelled on the fluorescence measurements routinely generated in flow cytometry. In a flow-cytometry experiment, cells from a heterogeneous sample pass individually through one or more laser beams, where fluorescently labelled antibodies can be used to target specific proteins bound to the surface membrane, allowing the fluorescence associated with each marker to be measured for individual cells, alongside properties derived from scattered light. Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS), therefore, is a specialised form of flow cytometry that can additionally sort cells into different populations based on these measured characteristics.

    Our synthetic dataset contains three continuous variables representing the fluorescence intensities associated with three cell-surface markers: CD3, CD19 and CD56, whose expression profiles are typically associated with T-, B- and natural killer cell types. The dataset is a sound demonstrative candidate for 3-D visualisation, as it allows us to explore how the three measurements jointly contribute to the structure and separation of different cell populations.

    Let's begin by reading our data into a Pandas DataFrame, using .head() to get a quick overview of its structure, and column headers.

    We can see from the first few rows that each cell has a numerical value attributed to CD3, CD19 and CD56 expression, together with its corresponding Cell_Type.

    Before plotting the data, let's check how many cells belong to each simulated population.

    NOTE

    The dataset contains three immune cell populations of different sizes. This is useful to bear in mind as we begin visualising the data, where a denser cluster of points may reflect a larger number of cells rather than greater biological variability.

    Constructing a 3-D scatter plot

    As aforementioned, with 3-D scatter plots, we add a third coordinate, z, allowing us to represent three measurements at a time. In the case of our flow cytometry data, this means that each simulated cell can be positioned in 3-dimensional space according to its expression of CD3, CD19 and CD56, simultaneously.

    Using Plotly Express' px.scatter_3d() function, we can draw a 3-D plot quite easily. The syntax closely resembles that of the px.scatter() function used in the previous lesson section, with the addition of a z argument for the third axis.

    Each point in the figure represents one simulated cell. Its position in three-dimensional space is determined by that cell's expression of the three markers; with its relative expression of all three markers contributing to each cell's position. Note that as with Plotly's 2-D scatter plots, mousing over each individual data point, reveals a hover label showing the plotted values for each of the cell surface markers.

    You might notice that there are regions of greater point density within the plot. However, since all cells currently share the same appearance, it is difficult to determine which regions correspond to particular immune cell populations. Further into the lesson, we will use additional visual properties such as colour to make these populations easier to identify.

    KEY TERMS

    Z-axis: The third spatial axis in a 3-D coordinate system. In our plot, the z-axis represents expression of the CD56 marker, alongside CD3 on the x-axis and CD19 on the y-axis.

    Navigating the 3-D space

    One of the main advantages of an interactive 3-D plot is that we are not restricted to a single viewing angle, and Plotly makes rotating the plot freely, very easy. Simply click and drag within the figure to rotate the data in three-dimensional space. You can also zoom in and out, pan across the figure and reset the camera using the Plotly toolbar.

    As you rotate the plot, notice the apparent 'shift' in relationships between the clusters. From some angles, two populations may appear well separated. From others, they may partially overlap or one population may obscure another. This is a phenomenon known as occlusion. The ability to change perspective is particularly important in biological imaging and multidimensional data analysis. Apparent separation or overlap can depend strongly on the direction from which a structure is viewed.

    KEY TERMS

    Occlusion: The visual hiding of one observation or group of observations behind another when three-dimensional data are projected onto a two-dimensional display.

    In addition to manual rotation with the mouse, we can also specify the initial Plotly camera position, programmatically. This can be useful when we want collaborators or readers to see the figure from a particular starting perspective. The eye coordinates describe the position of the virtual camera relative to the centre of the 3-D scene.

    TIP

    Alongside zooming, you can hold down the control key, while clicking and dragging your 3-D plot, to reposition it within your figure's borders.

    Enhancing a 3-D visualisation

    So far, we have deliberately ignored the known identity of each simulated cell and explored the structure of the data using marker expression alone.

    This gives us a useful exploratory question:

    Do the clusters that we can see in CD3-CD19-CD56 space correspond to biologically distinct immune cell populations?

    We can now reveal the Cell_Type information and examine whether the spatial structure of the data agrees with the simulated biological identities.

    Adding colour and size dimensions

    Perhaps the most obvious way to encode an additional categorical variable without changing the spatial position of the observations, is to alter its colour.

    Here, we will colour each cell according to its known Cell_Type. Using the color keyword argument, we can simply provide the name of a categorical variable in our data. Plotly will automatically assign a different colour to each unique data category present within that variable.

    The three spatial clusters can now be related directly to their immune cell identities. Remember that, broadly:

    • T cells tend to occupy a relatively CD3-high region, reflecting greater cell-surface expression of CD3

    • B cells tend to occupy a relatively CD19-high region, reflecting greater expression of CD19

    • NK cells tend to occupy a relatively CD56-high region, reflecting greater expression of CD56

    IMPORTANT

    These assignments loosely reflect the way lineage-associated markers are used in real immunophenotyping. However, real flow cytometry rarely identifies a cell type from a single marker in isolation. Cell populations are typically identified using combinations of markers, interpreted within the context of an appropriate gating strategy.

    Notice that assigning Cell_Type to the color argument also tells Plotly Express to create a separate trace for each cell population. Populations can thus be individually shown or hidden using the legend, just as we saw with categorical colour, earlier in this lesson.

    Plotly can also map a numerical variable to marker size. For demonstrative purposes, we can map CD3 expression to the size of each point. This creates an additional visual encoding of the same variable already displayed on the x-axis.

    Notice how the centres of some clusters appear almost white. This is a consequence of overplotting and transparency rather than a feature of the underlying biology.

    Since each plotted point represents an individual simulated cell, and we have set the marker opacity to 0.7, each point is now partially transparent. In densely populated regions, many markers are rendered such that they stack on top of one another. Plotly's 3-D renderer blends these overlapping semi-transparent points, producing the bright, indistinct regions visible near the centres of each cluster.

    Concurrently, since marker sizes now also encode CD3 expression, cells with higher CD3 expression are represented by larger points, which is particularly apparent within the T-cell population. However, these larger markers also increase the amount of overlap between neighbouring observations.

    This demonstrates an important limitation of adding further visual dimensions: even though position, colour and size allow us to represent several properties simultaneously, increasing the amount of information encoded within a plot can also make parts of the underlying distribution more difficult to see.

    Improving our visualisation

    Since the dense centres of our clusters are becoming difficult to distinguish, an immediate improvement that comes to mind would be to encircle each point in a line border. However, unlike Plotly's two-dimensional scatter plots, scatter_3d does not support drawing an outline around each marker. Instead, we can improve the figure by making the markers smaller and slightly less transparent.

    As we are mapping CD3 expression to marker size, we can use the size_max argument to limit the maximum size that any point can reach. This preserves the relative size differences between cells while reducing overplotting.

    NOTE: While Plotly Express does provide a size_max argument, it interestingly has no equivalent size_min. Observations with very low values can therefore appear as extremely small markers when size is mapped to a continuous variable, and you may need to zoom into the plot to see them clearly. This is one potential drawback of using marker size to encode data: small values can become difficult to perceive or compare.

    Increasing the opacity slightly also reduces the washed-out appearance produced when many semi-transparent markers are rendered together. The result should make individual observations and the internal structure of each population easier to distinguish.

    TIP

    To make up for Plotly's lack of a size_min argument and to better see the points in the cluster centres, you can click the 'zoom' button in the top right of the figure, clicking and dragging downward in the figure canvas, in order to zoom in. The white clouds will eventually come into focus as individual points. You can also zoom in and out of your plot by placing the mouse somewhere on the figure, and scrolling with your mouse (using either a scroll wheel or a similar gesture on a trackpad, for instance).

    Quantifying what we see

    As is apparent, the coloured 3-D plot suggests clear differences in marker expression between the three immune cell populations. While visualisation can be an excellent aid, apparent separation in a figure alone should not be treated as quantitative evidence.

    It might be tempting, for instance, to propose that T cells have higher CD3 expression than B cells or NK cells in this dataset. However, before applying any statistical test, we can begin by summarising the marker-expression distributions, numerically.

    Let's start by calculating the median expression of each marker within a given cell population. We can do this using the Pandas .median() method.

    NOTE

    Calculating the median is often useful when summarising measurements that may contain skewed distributions or extreme observations. In flow cytometry, the term median fluorescence intensity (MFI) is, in fact, commonly used to summarise the fluorescence distribution of a cell population. Our synthetic marker values are arbitrary rather than calibrated fluorescence measurements, but the same underlying principle still applies.

    The Pandas DataFrame above allows us to check whether the patterns suggested by our 3-D visualisation are also reflected numerically. Let's use a box plot to compare the distribution of CD3 expression across our three cell populations. In keeping with the plots we've done in this and the previous section of this lesson, let's use Plotly for this, using its px.box function.

    As this is a Plotly plot, it retains the same informative interactivity that we have been acquainting ourselves with. If you hover your cursor over the boxes, you will see the exact quartiles, as well as the median values that we calculated in the previous step, in the hover label.

    The box plot makes the distribution of CD3 expression within each population easier to compare directly, rather than the 3-D figure. The take-home message here is that exploratory visualisation and focused quantitative comparison can call for different representations of the same data. We would like to encourage your own thinking and playfulness here when approaching your data, as the choice of box plotting here is sensible given the nature of the data at hand.

    • A 3-D plot was useful for displaying and uncovering multidimensional structure

    • But ultimately, a 2-D plot was the more sound choice for comparing a specific variable between defined groups
    Whilst a statistical test could be used to formally investigate differences between populations, the appropriate test would depend on the experimental design, distribution of the measurements and scientific line of investigation being pursued.

    Customising the 3-D axes

    When working with 3-D plots in Plotly, the properties that define the three-dimensional plotting space are grouped within a component called the scene. We can customise these properties using the scene argument within Plotly's update_layout() method.

    KEY TERMS

    The scene controls features specific to the 3-D environment, including the x-, y- and z-axes, their relative proportions and the position of the virtual camera through which we view the data. Aspect ratio is also controlled via the plot's scene.

    Since the scene contains several components, including the x-, y- and z-axes, each of these are specified and supplied in a nested dictionary. Within this, we can provide a separate dictionary containing the settings for each axis.

    For example, we can replace abbreviated variable names with more informative axis labels, define axis ranges and adjust the relative proportions of the 3-D space. The nested scene dictionary contains a separate dictionary for each axis, allowing us to customise the axes, independently.

    NOTE

    Scene adjustments are not merely aesthetic. Changing the scale or proportions of an axis can influence how strongly patterns, distances and separation between observations appear in three dimensions.

    Let's start by simply labelling the x-, y- and z-axes.

    We can also control the relative proportions of the three axes. By default, Plotly uses aspectmode="auto" to determine the proportions of a 3-D scene automatically. We can instead specify how we want the axes to be scaled.

    • Setting aspectmode="cube" gives the x-, y- and z-axes equal physical lengths within the plotting area, regardless of the numerical range represented by each axis.
    This can produce a visually balanced figure, but it may distort our perception of distances and spatial relationships if the three variables span substantially different ranges.
    • Alternatively, aspectmode="data" scales the physical lengths of the axes in proportion to their data ranges.
    As CD3, CD19 and CD56 are represented on the same measurement scale in our synthetic dataset, this allows differences along each axis to be represented using a consistent visual scale.

    In other words, a difference of one fluorescence unit is represented by approximately the same physical distance along each axis, helping to preserve the geometry of our data.

    When to opt for 2-D over 3-D visualisations

    So far, we have seen that plotting CD3, CD19 and CD56 simultaneously allows us to explore the organisation of our three simulated immune cell populations in a single 3-D space. However, more dimensions do not necessarily make a visualisation more informative.

    Unless viewed using a virtual reality headset, a 3-D figure displayed on a flat computer screen is ultimately still projected onto a two-dimensional surface. This introduces some important limitations:

    • Distances can be difficult to judge

    • Points can obscure one another

    • The apparent separation between populations can change as we rotate the figure
    It is therefore important to question whether visualising your data in 3-D is actually adding anything meaningful. If not, a simpler 2-D visualisation may communicate the relationships in your data more clearly. That's not to discourage you from plotting your data in 3-D to explore it, but also to cement in your mind that 3-D is not always the answer.

    Let's consider a specific question relative to the synthetic data we've been using, in this section of the lesson.

    Can we distinguish the simulated B-cell and NK-cell populations from their marker expression, alone?

    From our earlier exploration, we know that B cells tend to have greater CD19 expression, while NK cells tend to have greater CD56 expression. If these are the two marker dimensions most relevant to our question, we may not need CD3 to visualise their separation. Let's therefore examine only CD19 and CD56.

    In this particular 2-D projection, the distinction between the B-cell and NK-cell populations is immediately visible. While we lose the advantage of displaying CD3 expression on the same plot, we can now more meaningfully compare cells along the CD19 and CD56 axes without needing to rotate the figure or interpret depth.

    Importantly, we have not changed the observations themselves. We have simply chosen to visualise the two dimensions that are most relevant to our particular question.

    CD3 expression may still contain useful biological information, but it is not necessary to resolve the distinction between B- and NK-cells. Breaking the problem down into lower-dimensional comparisons can be favourable.

    Let's next consider a different question.

    Can we distinguish all three simulated cell populations using their marker-expression profiles?

    Here, removing one marker may have a greater cost. Let's examine CD3 and CD19, while omitting CD56.

    This projection retains enough information to distinguish all three simulated cell populations. T cells tend towards relatively high CD3 expression, B cells towards relatively high CD19 expression and NK cells occupy a region in which both markers are comparatively low.

    However, we have removed CD56, so we can no longer see an important feature that characterises the NK-cell population in our simulated dataset: its relatively high CD56 expression.

    REMEMBER

    This illustrates an important trade-off when reducing dimensionality. A 2-D projection may preserve enough information to distinguish populations clearly, while still concealing biologically meaningful variation that exists along the omitted dimension.

    This is an important principle to bear in mind when we approach classical machine learning in the next scryptIQ course module. In these lessons, we will encounter datasets with many more features, or dimensions, than we can readily visualise or intuitively comprehend.

    We will explore dimensionality reduction as an unsupervised transformation technique that represents high-dimensional data using a smaller number of dimensions, while attempting to preserve important information and structure. This can make complex datasets easier to visualise, interpret and, in some cases, analyse computationally.

    As we have seen here, however, reducing dimensionality inevitably changes the representation of our data. We must therefore consider not only how much information is retained, but also what information may be lost or obscured.

    Summary

    In this section of Data Processing 4, we explored how 3-D visualisations can be used to investigate relationships between three continuous variables, simultaneously. Using a synthetic dataset, we constructed interactive 3-D scatter plots with Plotly, navigating the three-dimensional space, using colour and marker size to encode additional information and customise the axes. We also explored the scene, and the 'virtual camera' and geometry of the 3-D space that are encoded within it. We also considered some of the limitations of 3-D visualisation on a flat screen, including occlusion, perspective and the additional complexity involved in interpreting depth on a two-dimensional screen. By comparing 2-D and 3-D representations of our data, we also explored an important principle of scientific visualisation: adding dimensions can reveal otherwise hidden structure, but the most useful visualisation is not necessarily the one containing the most dimensions. Instead, our choice of dimensions should be guided by the structure of our data and, most importantly, the scientific question we are trying to answer.
