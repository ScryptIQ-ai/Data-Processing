    Masking and segmentation DHP

    Learning Objectives

    1 Understand why RGB is not always ideal for colour-based segmentation
    2 Convert colour images from RGB to HSV
    3 Interpret hue, saturation, and value in a biological context
    4 Create masks to isolate necrotic lesions from healthy tissue
    5 Quantify segmented regions using pixel-based measurements

    Introduction

    In this section of the lesson, we aim to apply image segmentation and masking, practically. We will demonstrate both RGB-based and HSV-based colour masking techniques. Relating to the latter, the HSV colour space that we introduced previously will be used as an anchor for exploring why RGB may sometimes be an unreliable, cumbersome choice for masking and segmenting certain colour images. In such cases, we will look at how HSV can often be easily used to isolate dominant hues, and the saturation and values of these, within an image. The focus of the main example given will be leaves photographed against a black background: some leaves are healthy, while others have necrotic lesions.

    KEY TERMS

    Segmentation is the process of partitioning an image into distinct, meaningful regions. Dividing an image up into areas containing signal and background, for example, would qualify as a segmentation of that image.

    Leaf necrosis example

    For our next demonstration, let's introduce our dataset. We will be using publicly available images of leaves photographed against a black background; with a mix of healthy and partially necrotic leaves. Healthy leaves appear relatively uniform in colour and texture across their surface, and tend to be bright green. However, when dehydrated, the plant will produce leaves with brown, crispy and dry tips and edges, denoting marginal necrosis, sometimes called 'leaf scorch'. These lesions may vary in texture and colour depending on how advanced the necrosis is - anything from pale yellow, turgid leaf tissue, through to darker brown lesions, with a dry and crunchy texture.

    Photographically, thus, our challenge is isolating green from yellow-brown regions of different intensities. This is where the HSV colour space offers an immediate thresholding advantage. When viewed as RGB images, the spectrum of browns does not align or correlate to a simple primary quantity; instead, they exist on a sliding scale of ever-shifting ratios of red, green and blue pixel intensities. When thinking of these same necrotic lesions from the perspective of an HSV colour space, A small range of hues will capture most browns, with a similarly narrow range of saturation values dictating how vivid or pallid these colours appear; and lastly a value range will capture how bright or dark these colours are.

    This does not make the problem entirely simple, since leaf damage rarely progresses in a perfectly uniform way. Between clearly healthy and clearly necrotic tissue, there may be transitional regions with mixed colour, partial dehydration, or subtle gradients in brightness and texture. Nevertheless, HSV gives us a much stronger starting point for thresholding than RGB alone, and is well suited to this kind of exploratory biological image segmentation.

    NOTE

    HSV- or RGB-based thresholding and masking are relatively simple segmentation approaches, and they are not as exhaustive or flexible as more advanced, machine-learning models such as clustering or Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). These often cope better with subtle colour variation, complex texture, uneven lighting and ambiguous boundaries.

    However simple colour-based masking remains very useful because it is fast and easy to compute and interpret. When processing large numbers of images on a domestic-spec computer, this can be a major practical advantage.

    IMPORTANT

    As established earlier in the lesson, attempting to threshold a specific colour using raw RGB ratios is extremely difficult. A colour like brown comprises a complex mixture of moderate red and green intensities, which makes it challenging to set reliable numerical boundaries using RGB alone.

    To demonstrate this at a glance, let's plot frequency histograms for the red, green and blue channels in a healthy leaf, versus a necrotic leaf. We have selected one image from each category, which show leaves that are very obviously healthy, and unhealthy, respectively.

    TIP

    As numerical arrays, remember that the third dimension stores the three colour channels. Thus, red is accessed by slicing into the array with index 0, green with index 1 and blue with index 2.

    These RGB histograms show that both the healthy and necrotic leaves contain broad and greatly overlapping distributions of red, green and blue intensities. Since the marginal necrosis is yellow/brown, there isn't a hugely obvious point at which to threshold and mask the necrotic areas away from the healhty regions.

    • In the necrotic leaf the distributions become broader and shift in a more complex way across all three channels, reflecting the introduction of yellow-brown lesions.
    Brown and yellow necrotic tissue are not represented by a single RGB channel, but by different mixtures of red, green and blue intensities that overlap considerably with those of healthy tissue.

    RGB masking of background Before we take the next measure to isolate the necrotic lesions, we will firstly separate the leaf tissue from the dark photographic background. Since the background is relatively dark and the leaf is bright, this would be a good case for simple RGB thresholding. Since we have eyeballed our images from the previews we generated on our 2x5 grid earlier, we know that the leaves are generally consistently placed in the middle of the frame. Thus, we can now write a function to estimate the background intensity by sampling the *bottom rows* of the image. This function will therefore estimate the RGB values of the **background** by taking the median red, green and blue intensities from the bottom 10 rows of the image, which are assumed to contain mostly black background. We can then define a pixel as 'leaf' if any of its RGB channels exceed this background estimate by a specified `OFFSET`, to capture a bit of variance around our specified background intensity ranges; or if its total brightness across combined R, G and B channels (which we'll control with `SUM_OFFSET`) is significantly higher. This ensures we capture all parts of the leaf, even pale or yellowing regions that might not stand out in a single channel. In the function, we use: - The variable `any_channel_high`, which becomes `True` whenever *at least one* of the three channels is sufficiently *brighter* than the background. - The variable `total_brightness`, which becomes `True` whenever the overall brightness of the pixel, across *all three channels together*, is sufficiently *above* background. The function then returns a **Boolean mask**, where: - `True` means the pixel is considered part of the leaf - `False` means it is treated as background Using both tests together makes the mask a little more flexible: it can pick up pixels that are strong in one channel, as well as pixels that may not stand out strongly in a single channel but are clearly brighter overall.

    Next, we can call our leaf_mask_rgb function to apply this mask. By inverting our Boolean mask (so that True becomes False), we can select all non-leaf pixels and set their RGB values to become pure black (0, 0, 0).

    To do this, we'll define a new function called apply_mask, which will apply the Boolean mask to each image by keeping the detected leaf pixels unchanged, and setting everything else to crush down to black. In doing so, it removes the background and leaves us with a cleaner set of images from which the leaf tissue can be analysed more directly.

    NOTE

    We make use of the .copy() method here, to create a copy of our images to which to apply the mask, so that we do not overwrite the original dataset. This is important, as we may find that our threshold values need a bit of adjusting with each call of these functions, and so we don't want to ruin the original images with 'mistakes', if we don't get it right the first time.

    TIP

    Remember that ~ is the negation operator: it puts a 'not' in front of the logic. In our example, it means 'capture everything that is not in mask, and overwrite its pixel intensities to be black ([0, 0, 0]).

    HSV thresholding

    With the background removed, we can focus entirely on the leaf tissue. We have overstated our reasoning behind our usage of the HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) colour space for this segmentation task and so, in this section, we will demonstrate this.

    By converting our image into HSV, we can define 'brown' simply by its hue, rather than guessing mixed ratios of red, green and blue.

    To start off, we will use Matplotlib's rgb_to_hsv() function (imported from its colors module), which expects RGB values to be scaled between 0.0 and 1.0. Once converted, we can define logical boundaries for brown and yellow hues, as well as darker regions, to isolate the necrotic tissue.

    Quantification

    So far, we have focused on the visual representation of detected marginal necrosis. We may also wish to describe them, quantitatively. One of the main advantages of image segmentation is that, once regions of interest have been isolated, they can be treated as measurable data rather than as visualised regions, alone. In this case, a straightforward first step is to count how many pixels belong to the leaf as a whole, then count how many of those are classified as necrotic, and express that value as a percentage of the total leaf area.

    We then have an easily interpreted way to quantify necrosis, for re-integration of this information into a larger tabular dataset, for example. Although it is only an approximation, and depends on the quality of the thresholding and masking steps that came before it, it is still extremely useful as a fast analysis of our images, allowing us to compare leaves more objectively, or even rank them by apparent severity of damage.

    REMEMBER

    The logic we will make use of, makes use of the enumerate() method, introduced in our Python Fundamentals module. enumerate() here iterates over our list of necrotic images, capturing both each image as a numerical array, and its index, both of which can be used to label the leaf, and perform a simple mathematical percentage calculation.

    Summary

    In the final section of this lesson, we introduced image segmentation and masking as a way of turning leaf photographs into analysable numerical data. We began by loading and visualising a small dataset of healthy and necrotic leaves, then used simple RGB-based masking to separate the leaf from its dark background. We then motivated the move from RGB to HSV colour space, showing why necrotic yellow-brown lesions are awkward to isolate using raw red, green and blue values alone, and why hue, saturation and value provide a more intuitive basis for colour-based thresholding.

    From there, we built Boolean masks to detect candidate necrotic regions, applied these masks to produce composite visual outputs, and finally quantified visible damage by measuring and calculating the percentage of each leaf classified as necrotic. In summary, we demonstrated how thresholding, masking, visualisation, and simple pixel-based measurement can be combined into a practical workflow for exploratory biological image analysis.
